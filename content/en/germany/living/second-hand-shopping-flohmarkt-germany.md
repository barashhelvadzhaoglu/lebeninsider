You don't have to buy everything brand new in Germany. Second-hand is very common here and makes things much easier in terms of purchasing power. Let me share my own experiences.

## Setting up a home with Kleinanzeigen

We came from Turkey by car and stayed in a temporary apartment for a while; we had no furniture. We followed the free listings on Kleinanzeigen. We met our basic needs like a crib, toddler bed, and a 40–50 inch TV this way; we set up part of our house like this.

People give away their items for free with the mindset that "giving is better than throwing away." I think it's a great thing; it feels like solidarity among people. Those moving from Turkey sometimes bring all their belongings. It's also possible to bring some and find some here this way.

We learned a few things:

- **Demand is very high.** Items are gone within half an hour or an hour after being posted; you have to be fast.
- **Outskirts are more affordable.** The same items are cheaper—sometimes even free—at houses 50–100 km away from the city because people don't want to drive that far to pick them up. If the same item is inside Munich, they usually want money for it. We went and got some of our furniture this way.

## Sharing boxes (Verschenkbox)

There are small huts and boxes at certain points in the city (like Verschenkbox). People leave items like books, clothes, plates, dishes, and cups here; anyone who needs them can take them.

## Flohmarkt: neighborhood flea market

There is a flea market concept here: **Flohmarkt**. A specific neighborhood agrees among themselves; one day a week, everyone puts a table in front of their house. They arrange the products they no longer use at home and want to sell on top of the table. People from surrounding areas come and look at the tables: what products are there, what are the prices. Bargaining takes place. If you get lucky, you can buy very affordable products.

We usually focus on children's items. Our kids ask for Paw Patrol, so we bought Paw Patrol toys; things like toys and decorations.

Once, our home TV broke down. I bought a 40-inch TV for **2 euros** at a Flohmarkt 500 meters away from my house. It was very heavy, I couldn't carry it by hand; the seller had a tool for carrying, and I temporarily used that to bring it home. The TV still works.

Earlier, I had also bought a TV from Kleinanzeigen for 5 euros. That one had probably reached the end of its life, and it broke down after a while. These things happen with second-hand items.

## Why I don't buy some things brand new

Since the kids are small, I deliberately don't buy certain things brand new; kids can break or ruin them. While living in Turkey, my son had broken the TV. After these experiences, I make sure not to buy some things brand new.

I also bought a second-hand iPhone 16 for myself through MediaMarkt. You don't have to buy it brand new.

## Second-hand clothing

They even sell second-hand clothes. There are second-hand clothing stores in the city; you can find a 100-euro sweater for 8, 10, or 20 euros. Many people usually shop this way.

## In short

I think buying second-hand products and having this experience is a good thing. If you've just moved, keep an eye on Kleinanzeigen, the Flohmarkt in your neighborhood, and the sharing boxes; setting up a home becomes much easier and cheaper.
