When November arrives in Germany, you'll see groups of children walking through the streets in the evening carrying glowing lanterns and singing songs. This is the **St. Martin** tradition, and for kids attending daycare (Kita) or primary school, it is one of the most anticipated days of the year.

If your child goes to Kita in Germany, you'll most likely take part in a lantern procession this year. Here is what you need to know:

## Who is St. Martin?

According to legend, Martin was a Roman soldier who lived in the 4th century. On a freezing winter day, he saw a shivering beggar on the road, cut his cloak in two with his sword, and gave half of it to him. Later, he became a bishop and was recognized as a saint.

That is why St. Martin is celebrated in Germany as a day focused on **sharing and helping others**. This story is also shared with children at daycare.

## When does it take place?

The actual day is **November 11th** (a Wednesday in 2026). However, lantern processions can take place on different days depending on each Kita, school, or church community—usually during the week of November 11th, or the week before/after, in the evening once it gets dark.

Your Kita usually announces the date and time a few weeks in advance.

## What happens during the lantern procession?

1. **Making the lantern:** Children usually craft their lanterns (Laterne) at daycare or at home with their families using cardboard, colored paper, and glue.
2. **The walk:** Once it gets dark, children walk through the neighborhood or a park with their lanterns, accompanied by their families.
3. **The songs:** While walking, everyone sings traditional Laterne songs. The most famous ones are "Ich geh mit meiner Laterne" and "Laterne, Laterne, Sonne, Mond und Sterne."
4. **In some places:** A rider portraying "St. Martin" on horseback leads the way, a bonfire (Martinsfeuer) is lit at the end, and a short play reenacts the cloak-sharing story.
5. **Afterward:** Warm tea, Kinderpunsch (non-alcoholic punch), sweet pastries, or pretzels (Brezeln) are served while families chat.

## Practical tips for families

- **Learn the songs beforehand.** The Kita usually sends out the lyrics. Listening to them a few times at home with your child will make things easier for both of you during the walk; it's also a great opportunity for some German practice.
- **Use an electric lantern stick instead of a candle.** These days, people usually use a battery-powered LED stick (Laternenstab) inside the lantern instead of a real candle—it's much safer. You can find them for a few euros at supermarkets and drugstores. Bring spare batteries.
- **Dress warmly.** November evenings are chilly, and the procession often lasts 30 to 60 minutes.
- **Ready-made lanterns work too.** If you don't have time, supermarkets sell ready-made lanterns; but a lantern crafted together at Kita has a special meaning for children.

## Martinsgans: The roast goose tradition

There is also a tradition for adults: **Martinsgans** (St. Martin's goose). Around November 11th, many restaurants offer a roast goose menu served with red cabbage and potato dumplings (Knödel). It is very common in Bavaria, and popular restaurants often require booking well in advance.

## In summary

St. Martin is a wonderful opportunity for families with children in Germany to connect with the local community and meet other daycare parents. For newcomer families like ours, traditions like these also help our children feel that they belong here.
