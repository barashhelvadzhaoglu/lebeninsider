After moving to Germany, I had to relearn how to do grocery shopping. If you go with habits from Turkey, you either end up facing empty shelves, waiting in a long checkout line, or finding the store doors locked on a Sunday. In this post, I’ve rounded up what we've learned at the supermarket.

## Staple Prices Are Pretty Much the Same Across All Supermarkets

This was the first thing that caught my attention. For basic staple items like cheese, bread, milk, water, fruit, and vegetables, prices are generally the same no matter which supermarket you walk into. You don't have to wander from store to store thinking "milk is cheaper at that other place" like back in Turkey.

What really makes the difference are the discounts (which I explain below). On top of that, the price gap between eating out and buying groceries is enormous.

## Groceries vs. Eating Out?

Eating out in Munich is generally expensive. You realize this best when comparing it to grocery store prices. In the supermarket, I roughly see prices like this:

- **1 kg of minced meat:** €10–€12
- **1 kg of chicken breast:** €8–€10

Yet the smallest burger meal at McDonald's costs around the same amount. In other words, for the price of a single fast-food meal for one person, you can buy enough meat to feed a whole family for several meals. Even at places considered cheap for takeout, like Asian noodle shops, meals start around €10; a simple Margherita pizza also starts at €10.

Because of this, people bring their own lunch to work; they also bring sandwiches and food along when visiting parks. It’s completely normal here, and nobody finds it odd. When we go on trips, we also pack plenty of food with us.

*Prices are based on my own observations; they vary by city, supermarket chain, and time period.*

## Everything Is Packaged

Another difference: there is almost nothing sold loose or unpackaged in supermarkets. Fruits and vegetables are usually sold pre-packaged. Legumes are packaged, and even hardware supplies are packaged. The habit of picking just the exact amount you want from an open display—common back in Turkey—hardly exists here; you often end up having to buy more than you actually need.

## Saving 30–40% If You Follow Discounts

Supermarkets run discounts constantly. For many items, the promotional discount cycles back every 2–3 weeks. If you track them well and buy non-perishables when they're on sale—say, enough to last 2–3 months—you can do your general shopping 30–40% cheaper than regular shelf prices.

A few types of discounts we regularly keep an eye on:

- **Weekly discounts:** Supermarkets feature rotating discounted products each week in their printed brochures and mobile apps.
- **Bakery section:** In some supermarkets, one or two bakery items are sold at a discount each week. I also mentioned supermarket bakeries in my post on [day trip preparation with kids](/en/germany/travel/day-trip-with-kids-packing-stroller/).
- **Items nearing expiration date:** Especially meat, fish, and poultry are discounted by 20–30% when approaching their best-before date. If you plan to cook or consume them the same day or the next day, it's a great opportunity.

## When to Go?

If you pick the wrong hour, you either face huge crowds or completely wiped-out shelves.

- **Early morning:** Supermarkets are very busy in the morning. Older residents come in early to pick up fresh tomatoes, peppers, produce, and deli items. Friday and Saturday mornings get particularly packed; however, the items are fresh and shelves are fully stocked.
- **Late afternoon (17:00–19:00):** People leave work around 16:00–17:00 and head straight to the store. You can expect significant lines at the registers during these hours.
- **After 19:00:** Whenever I go to a supermarket closing at 20:00 after 19:00, almost all the shelves are empty; daily vegetables, fruits, eggs, and milk are often completely sold out.

Most shops close at 20:00 at the latest (here in Bavaria). Some supermarkets located near subway and bus hubs open at 7:00; those are great for grabbing something on your way to work.

## Saturday Shopping: Plan for at Least 3 Days

Supermarkets are closed on Sundays. And if you can't swing by a grocery store on Monday morning before work, you need to do your Saturday shopping keeping **Saturday, Sunday, and Monday** in mind. Because of this, I especially recommend doing your weekend grocery shopping early in the day; shelves are stocked and you avoid the late-afternoon rush.

If you urgently need something on Sunday, kiosks (Spätis / Kiosks) and gas station convenience shops remain open. I covered other practical tips regarding Sundays and public holidays in [this post](/en/germany/living/sundays-and-public-holidays-in-germany/).

## In Short

- Price differences on staples are small; the real savings come from sales and discounts.
- Eating out is expensive; people pack food for work and the park.
- Stock up on items during discounts, and look for products nearing expiration.
- Go early in the morning; avoid the 17:00–19:00 rush and shopping after 19:00.
- Plan Saturday groceries with Sunday and Monday in mind as well.
