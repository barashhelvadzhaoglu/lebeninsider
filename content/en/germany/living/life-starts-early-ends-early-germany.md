One of the quickest things someone moving from Turkey notices in Germany is the rhythm of daily life. The day starts very early here and winds down early in the evening. In this post, I share this rhythm and a few related observations.

## The day starts at 8 and ends at 5

Many places open at 8 in the morning and close at 5 in the evening; you can generally think of typical working hours as 8 to 5.

Some supermarkets, especially those near subway and bus stops, open at 7:00 AM. Very few shops stay open after 8:00 PM; staying open any later requires special permission. In other words, life here starts early and ends early.

This has a big impact on everyday life: supermarkets get packed in the late afternoon with people shopping after work, and after 7 PM, the shelves start looking empty. I covered the grocery side in detail in my post on [grocery shopping in Germany](/en/germany/living/grocery-shopping-food-prices-germany/).

## Seniors are fully part of daily life

Germany has a significant elderly population. But it's not like in Turkey: here, people are still driving cars at 80 or 90 years old. They remain actively involved in life throughout the day; they go to cafes and restaurants, eat out, and enjoy a cup of tea or coffee. Because of this, the streets, cafes, and supermarkets are never empty during the day.

The busiest hours in supermarkets in the morning are when seniors head out for groceries. They come early to buy fresh vegetables, fruits, and deli items.

In the late afternoon, I often see them meeting in front of apartment buildings or in parks. Some can truly barely walk. For this, they have wheeled walking frames (called a **Rollator** in German); it looks like a small trolley that they hold onto while walking, and when they stop, they can sit down on it. I think it's a wonderful system: even someone with mobility challenges can get out of the house and meet friends at the park.

This also connects to what I discussed in my [transportation post](/en/germany/living/cars-traffic-turkey-vs-germany/): sidewalks, pedestrian walkways, and elevators are all built with people with disabilities and the elderly in mind.

## Utility bills are calculated once a year, not monthly

In Turkey, someone comes by every month to read the meter, and you pay for whatever you consumed that month. That's not how it works here: expenses like heating and electricity are calculated annually; no one comes to read the meter every month.

The system roughly works like this: you pay a fixed advance payment each month (*Abschlag*). At the end of the year, a calculation is made based on your actual consumption; if you used less, the difference is refunded, and if you used more, you make an additional payment. For heating and ancillary costs in your rent, this statement is called the **Nebenkostenabrechnung**.

You need to be prepared for this during your first year: if you kept your home very warm throughout the winter, an unexpected additional bill might arrive at the end of the year.

## In short

- Work, school, and public offices start early in the morning; shops close after 8:00 PM.
- Seniors are an active part of daily life throughout the day, and the city is structured accordingly.
- Heating and electricity are paid via monthly advance installments, while the actual balancing calculation happens once a year.
