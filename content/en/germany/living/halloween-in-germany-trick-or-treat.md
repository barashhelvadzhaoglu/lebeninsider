As October 31 approaches, pumpkins, costumes, and bags of candy start filling up the supermarkets. Halloween is originally an American tradition, but it is becoming more and more widespread in Germany every year, especially among families with children. For someone coming from Turkey, it can be both fun and a bit confusing; that's why I've put together what you need to know.

## How is Halloween celebrated in Germany?

- **Costumes:** Kids dress up in costumes like witches, skeletons, and vampires. You can find costumes in supermarkets, drugstores (*Drogerie* like dm and Rossmann), and toy stores.
- **"Süßes oder Saures!":** The German equivalent of "trick or treat." Kids go door-to-door in groups in the late afternoon and early evening asking for candy.
- **Pumpkins:** Carved pumpkins (*Kürbis*) are placed in front of doorways. In the fall, plenty of pumpkins are sold in supermarkets and at local farm stands.
- **Parties:** Costume parties are organized for adults in bars and clubs.

## Not every door gets knocked on

Here is an important nuance: Halloween is not something everyone celebrates in Germany. Older people in particular, as well as some families, do not observe this tradition.

The general rule is: **Houses with decorated doors or windows, or with a pumpkin outside**, are considered open for trick-or-treating. If there are no decorations, kids usually skip that door. If you want to hand out candy, putting a small decoration or a pumpkin outside your door is enough.

If you are going out with kids:

- Head out early in the evening (just as dusk falls); people don't appreciate being disturbed late at night.
- In apartment buildings, head towards decorated doors instead of ringing everyone's bell.
- Always have an adult accompany them, and make sure they wear something reflective so they stay visible in the dark.

## The next day: November 1, Allerheiligen

The day after Halloween, **November 1, is a public holiday in Bavaria**: Allerheiligen (All Saints' Day). This day is a public holiday in Bavaria, Baden-Württemberg, North Rhine-Westphalia, Rhineland-Palatinate, and Saarland; supermarkets and shops are closed.

Allerheiligen is also a **"quiet day" (stiller Tag)**. People visit cemeteries and light candles on graves. Because of this, dancing and loud public entertainment are restricted in Bavaria; Halloween parties usually wrap up shortly after midnight.

October 31 is a public holiday as Reformation Day (*Reformationstag*) in some states (northern and eastern states like Hamburg, Bremen, Lower Saxony, and Saxony), but **not in Bavaria**.

In 2026, October 31 falls on a Saturday and November 1 falls on a Sunday; so this year Allerheiligen doesn't take away an extra working day anyway. Still, plan your Saturday grocery shopping accordingly. I explained other practical tips about Sundays and public holidays in [this post](/en/germany/living/sundays-and-public-holidays-in-germany/).

## In short

- Halloween does exist in Germany, but it isn't celebrated everywhere or by everyone.
- Decorated doors mean "you can ring"; don't push your luck with undecorated ones.
- In Bavaria, the next day (November 1) is a public holiday and a quiet day (*stiller Tag*).
