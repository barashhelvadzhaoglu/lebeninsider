Last year during Oktoberfest, we weren't in Munich, so this year was our very first Oktoberfest. In total, we went 4–5 times. Why so many times? Because you simply can't get to everything in a single day. During the daytime, I was at work and language class, and my wife was at her course; the kids would get out of school around 16:00–17:00, and we'd pick them up and head straight to the festival.

In this post, I've gathered what we learned for anyone considering going to Oktoberfest with kids. Oktoberfest is over for now, but if you're planning to go next year (it has been announced for September 18 – October 3 in 2027), it will come in handy.

## Oktoberfest in brief

The origins of Oktoberfest date back to 1810, to the wedding celebrations of Bavarian Crown Prince Ludwig and Princess Therese. That is also where the name of the festival grounds, Theresienwiese, comes from. Munich locals simply call the festival the **Wiesn**.

The Spring Festival (Frühlingsfest) is also held on the same grounds in springtime; I wrote about that in a [separate post](/en/germany/living/munich-spring-festival/).

## Getting there: public transit and plenty of walking

The festival grounds are massive; different U-Bahn (subway) lines run along the north, south, and east, and tram and S-Bahn (suburban train) lines pass close by as well. But even from the closest stop, you have to be prepared for a substantial walk. Even though distances look short on the map, progress is slow because crowds of people are flocking to the square in droves.

Approaching by bicycle or car is very difficult; you just can't find a clear route. Public transit is by far the most sensible option. Since people are drinking, everyone takes public transit on the way back anyway.

## Entrance and walking distance

You can only enter and exit the grounds through specific designated points. There is a security check at the entrance; if you carry a bag, they check inside. There are paid luggage storage points (like a cloakroom) for large bags; you pick up your belongings when leaving.

Just to reach the entrance by walking around the perimeter, you easily walk at least 500 meters. Once inside, wandering around "looking left and looking right," you quickly end up walking 2–3 km. Just to look around and take in what's there, you need to cover at least 5 km on foot. With kids, this becomes even more exhausting. On our first evening, all we did was stroll around and look.

## Stroller rules and our tip

According to the official rules:

- **On Saturdays and on October 3**, strollers and buggies are not allowed onto the festival grounds at all.
- **On other days**, you can enter with a stroller until 18:00.
- **Children under 6** cannot stay in the beer tents after 20:00, even if accompanied by their parents.

In practice, if you entered before 18:00, you can usually stay; occasionally, staff will warn you that "you need to leave." Make sure to check the rules on the official website before you go.

**Our tip:** We carry a bicycle lock on our stroller. Whenever we can't take the stroller inside somewhere, we lock it to a fence along the roadside and head in. Since there are so many bicycles in Germany, you usually have extra locks lying around at home anyway; that's how we put our spare lock to good use. Officially, there are also luggage storage points for strollers, and there is a dedicated **family area (Familienplatzl)** on the grounds: stroller parking, baby changing tables, and a beer garden where you can bring your own food and sit down.

The best time with kids is weekday lunchtimes and afternoons. During the festival, two Tuesdays are designated as **family days (Familientag)**; many rides and stalls offer discounted prices until 19:00.

## Rides, cash, and price differences

Lining both sides of the main avenue are the massive tents run by the breweries. Outside the main avenue, you'll find fairground rides: bumper cars, a Ferris wheel, and more high-thrill attractions. There are rides aimed at kids, but most are geared toward adults. The Spring Festival had more food and drink stalls; Oktoberfest leans more toward action-packed, adrenaline-heavy rides.

Two important points:

- **Bring cash.** Many places only take cash and do not accept cards. Because we ran low on cash, we found ourselves actively searching for stands that accepted card payments.
- **Prices vary widely.** The exact same ride can cost 8 euros at one spot, 6 euros at another, and 5 euros somewhere else. French fries might be 5 euros at one stall, 7 at another, and 8 at a third. It pays to walk around and scout before buying anything.

## Traditional attire (Tracht): last-minute bargaining

Germans without fail show up to Oktoberfest in their traditional attire (Tracht): an 80-year-old grandmother, a 50-year-old man, a 15-year-old girl. Men, women, everyone dresses up and accessorizes.

We left this to the absolute last minute. Our child's class was going to Oktoberfest together, and the kids were asked to wear traditional clothes; so we had no choice but to buy some. Near the Hauptbahnhof (central train station), I stumbled upon a seasonally rented shop that isn't normally a clothing store. The shopkeeper had apparently come all the way from Italy just for Oktoberfest to sell outfits.

- He asked 40 euros for the leather shorts (Lederhosen) and 20 euros for the shirt. I said, "I have two kids, let's agree on 100 euros total," and he accepted.
- A few days later, we returned with the kids and my wife. He calculated the bill as 120 euros; when I said "you said 100 the other day," he agreed again.
- My wife's outfit was 120 euros; when we bought a second piece, he gave a 20–30 euro discount. We probably could have bargained even a bit more.
- Payment was again in cash.

While we were trying clothes on, a steady stream of people walked in: people straight off work, tourists... They put the clothes on, say "looks great," stuff their regular clothes into a plastic bag, and head directly to the festival.

Prices fluctuate heavily depending on location. In central, high-traffic spots, some places ask around 100 euros even for outfits that look like cheap children's costumes. Keep an eye on quality, not just price.

## Inside the tent: 10,000 people standing on tables

We managed to get inside one of the massive tents once; we left the stroller outside. Disco-style music blasts inside; there are 10,000 people in the tent, and everyone is up on the benches and tables, dancing and singing along. If it were just one or two evenings, I could understand it, but to see every tent packed like this every single evening, with thousands of people eating, drinking, and celebrating, is truly a sight you won't easily find anywhere else.

Once it fills up inside, they close the doors and let nobody else in. In the evenings, reservations for the large tents are generally fully booked. During the day, however, school classes can visit; the tents offer children's menus (usually until the afternoon).

People eat proper, hearty meals at the tables, not just snacks. Normally at a beer hall, glasses are half a liter; here they are one liter (**Maß**). Oktoberfest beer also has a slightly higher alcohol content than usual: between 5.9% and 6.3% in 2026. The price of a Maß in 2026 was between 14.80 and 15.90 euros.

## My observations

- Germans always go to Oktoberfest in groups; there is no "I'll just go hang out by myself" mindset, and I saw very few people attending alone.
- Throughout the festival (about 2.5 weeks), in every corner of the city and at every hour of the day, you see people in Tracht heading to or coming back from the festival.
- It's not like going once and staying from morning till night; many people visit 3–5 times. There isn't much action during the day; the real crowd arrives in the evening. Between 18:00 and 19:00 you can hardly take a step; around 21:00–21:30 people start heading home, so they don't stay out excessively late.
- I can compare it to old-school town fairs and amusement parks in Turkey, but on an exponentially grander scale.

## In short

- Go on weekdays in the afternoon; no strollers are allowed on Saturdays.
- Bring cash, and compare prices before you buy.
- Be prepared for lots of walking; don't try to see everything in a single day with kids.
- Don't leave buying Tracht to the last minute; if you do, don't hesitate to negotiate.
