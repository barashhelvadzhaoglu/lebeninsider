When we moved to Germany, we decided to drive our own car instead of flying. We had a lot of luggage, and a connecting flight with two small children (then aged 1 and 5) did not appeal to us. We drove more than 2,000 km from Istanbul to Munich through Bulgaria, Romania, Hungary and Austria in **two days**. Here is our route and what helped us on the way.

## Our route

1. **Istanbul → Kapıkule:** the exit from Turkey. As it is an EU external border, passport and vehicle checks happen here.
2. **Bulgaria: Sofia → Vidin:** after Sofia there is a mountainous road north-west towards the Romanian border. We drove it at night; it was narrow, winding and foggy in places, the most tiring part of the trip. Drive it in daylight if you can.
3. **Vidin–Calafat bridge into Romania:** the bridge has a toll. In Romania we took the A1 motorway towards the Hungarian border (Arad / Nădlac).
4. **Hungary: one night in Budapest:** we stopped halfway in Budapest; we paid €80 for the night back then. Sleeping in a real bed made the second day much easier.
5. **Austria: Vienna → Linz → Passau:** on the last day we drove via Vienna and Linz and entered Germany at Passau.

## Why Romania and not Serbia?

The Serbian route looks shorter on the map. But we bought our green card insurance at the Bulgarian border as a cheap EU-only policy, and Serbia is not in the EU. So we kept the whole route inside the EU. I explain how in our [green card insurance post](/en/germany/expat/green-insurance-guide-save-money/).

Bulgaria and Romania are now part of the Schengen area, so after Kapıkule there are no regular border checks between Bulgaria, Romania, Hungary, Austria and Germany, although random checks can happen.

## Road tolls (vignettes)

Germany has no motorway toll for cars, but every other country on the way does:

- **Bulgaria:** electronic vignette
- **Romania:** rovinietă (electronic)
- **Hungary:** e-matrica
- **Austria:** digital or sticker vignette

You can buy them online or at border and petrol station sales points. Prices change every year, so check the official websites before you go. The fine for driving without one is far higher than the vignette itself.

## What worked for us

- **Driving at night:** we left at the children's bedtime, around 8–9 pm, and covered most of the distance while they slept. When we got tired, we stopped at a large, well-lit petrol station and slept in the car for a few hours.
- **Romanian roads:** we expected bad roads, but the A1 motorway was wide and well maintained, one of the easiest parts of the trip.
- **The Budapest stop:** splitting the two days in half was good for us and the kids.

## What was hard

- **LPG:** our car runs on LPG. After Budapest the pump nozzle no longer fitted, and we drove the last 800–900 km on petrol. If your car runs on LPG, read [our LPG adapter story](/en/germany/expat/europe-lpg-guide-honda-civic-experience/) first.
- **The mountain road after Sofia:** at night, foggy and winding. Much easier in daylight.

## What to bring

Passports, driving licence, vehicle registration, green card insurance, cash in euros, an LPG adapter set if needed, and food, water and pillows for the children. Buying the vignettes online before you leave saves time at the borders.

## In short

Driving from Istanbul to Munich can be easier than flying, especially for families with children and lots of luggage. We went via Romania with one night in Budapest and did it in two days. Our two biggest lessons: choose your route based on what your green card insurance covers, and don't forget the LPG adapter.
