When you become unemployed in Germany, the state supports you; that is true. But in return, they scrutinize every detail of your life, and you deal with a bureaucracy that requires you to shuttle back and forth between multiple institutions. In this post, I share what I went through during the period I was unemployed. My goal is to help anyone facing the same situation know what to prepare for.

*Note: I am sharing my personal experience; regulations vary depending on individual circumstances. Please consult the Agentur für Arbeit and Jobcenter for official information.*

## I found out I was being laid off via email

I received the news that I was going to be laid off via email. First, I went to the federal employment agency (**Agentur für Arbeit**); from there, they redirected me to the **Jobcenter**. When I went to the Jobcenter, they told me, "You aren't officially unemployed yet; you have to come and apply after your employment has actually ended."

My employment contract ended on a Friday; on Monday morning, I went straight there to submit my application.

> **What you need to know:** You must register as **"looking for work" (arbeitsuchend)** with the Agentur für Arbeit at least 3 months before your contract ends, or within 3 days of learning about the dismissal if you were notified later. If you miss this deadline, a penalty period (**Sperrzeit**) of up to one week with no unemployment benefits (Arbeitslosengeld) may be applied. The actual application at the Jobcenter, on the other hand, is done once you are officially unemployed.

## You have to document your entire life

After submitting the application, letters started arriving at my home; each one requested a different set of documents. I went in and handed them over. The Agentur für Arbeit has an online portal on its website; they set up a user account for me, allowing me to upload several documents directly through it.

Some of the requested documents:

- Rental agreement (*Mietvertrag*)
- Past pay slips (*Gehaltsabrechnungen*)
- Bank account statements and the exact balance in each account
- **Assets in Turkey:** Everything you own, including bank accounts back in Turkey
- Photocopies of IDs, passports, and residence permit cards (*Aufenthaltstitel*)
- Health insurance details

So yes, the state supports you; but first, they examine everything down to the smallest detail. Having these documents organized in advance in a folder (and stored digitally) will make your life much easier.

## Health insurance continues

Even when you are unemployed, your health insurance contributions continue to be paid. If you want to stay with the same statutory health insurance fund (**Krankenkasse**) you had while working, you can; I was insured with TK (Techniker Krankenkasse) and stayed with them.

While I was employed, my wife and children were covered under my family insurance (*Familienversicherung*). During the time we were unemployed, they insured my wife under her own name; once I found a new job, she was switched back under my coverage.

**An issue we encountered:** Because my Turkish and Bulgarian names are spelled differently, two separate records were created at the insurance provider. The Jobcenter was issuing payments under one name, while the insurance company was searching under the other; they told me, "We cannot find you, no payment has arrived." In the end, I explained that I have two official names and proved it with documents, after which they merged my profiles. If you have multiple names or different spellings across your documents, make sure to inform every agency right from the start.

## The Kindergeld mess: shuttling between three institutions

Child benefits (**Kindergeld**), which are paid to every family, are handled by a separate agency called the family benefits office (**Familienkasse**). The Jobcenter calculates the family's total monthly financial need and treats Kindergeld as part of your family's income in that calculation.

Let me give an example: Suppose the Jobcenter calculates your family's monthly need at €3,000. The child benefits that the Familienkasse is supposed to pay you (around €500 for our two children) are deducted from this calculation; in fact, sometimes the Familienkasse pays that amount directly to the Jobcenter rather than to you.

Our problem was this: The Familienkasse required a certificate stating that we had become unemployed through no fault of our own. We were supposed to obtain this document from the Agentur für Arbeit. We went to the Agentur; they told us, "You are not receiving benefits from us, go to the Jobcenter." The Jobcenter told us, "Only the Agentur can issue this document." I went back to the Familienkasse and said, "I cannot resolve this deadlock, please help me"; they gave me a new letter, and I went back to speak with the other two agencies once again. It turned into a drawn-out ordeal.

Because this issue could not be resolved quickly, the Jobcenter simply assumed we were already receiving the child benefits and **underpaid us by up to €500 every month**. Once the issue was finally settled, they paid back the missing amount; however, in the meantime, I had accumulated quite a bit of debt and had to clear it all afterward.

## Labor court and severance pay

I also went through a labor court (*Arbeitsgericht*) process regarding my dismissal, which I described in [a separate post](/en/germany/expat/fired-in-germany-labor-court/). As far as I understand, social assistance benefits are normally not approved until the labor court outcome is clear; however, they made an exception in my case. Because I did not have a termination letter with an original pen-and-ink signature, I obtained a written statement explaining that a lawsuit could not be formally filed, and I took that to the Jobcenter; that document allowed my benefits to start.

Afterward, they continuously asked, "What is the status of the labor court case?" I was regularly emailing my former employer, and I presented those sent emails as proof to the Jobcenter: "Look, I keep emailing them, but the company is not responding."

Eventually, the company responded and we reached a severance payment (*Abfindung*). But this severance pay did not really benefit us: the Jobcenter **divided the severance sum across six months** and treated it as monthly income for that period; our benefits were reduced accordingly. We filed an objection (*Widerspruch*), but they did not adjust their decision.

## Keeping a Minijob: part of your earnings stays with you

During the time I was unemployed, I continued working my [Minijob](/en/germany/living/work-life-turkey-vs-germany/) and reported it to the Jobcenter. They repeatedly asked for every single payslip from the Minijob as well.

The Jobcenter wants you to work, and to encourage employment, they do not deduct all of your earnings from your benefits. The allowances (*Freibeträge*) work roughly as follows:

- The first **€100** of your earnings is completely yours to keep.
- You keep **20%** of the portion between €100 and €520.
- You keep **30%** of the portion between €520 and €1,000.

For instance, if you earn €300 a month from a Minijob, approximately €140 stays directly in your pocket; the rest is deducted from your benefits. It might seem small, but it is better than nothing. On top of that, having a continuously paid payroll record in Germany can give you an advantage down the road.

*Note: Starting in mid-2026, Bürgergeld is gradually transitioning into a new framework called "Neue Grundsicherung"; the specific allowance rules may change.*

## Key takeaways

- **Register early:** As soon as you learn that you will be dismissed, register as looking for work (*arbeitsuchend*) with the Agentur für Arbeit immediately.
- **Documents:** Have your rental contract, past payslips, bank statements (including those in Turkey), ID cards, and residence permits ready.
- **Keep everything in writing:** Save every email sent to your employer or to government offices; they serve as critical proof.
- **Name discrepancies:** If your name has different official spellings across different countries, notify all agencies right at the start.
- **Keep working:** A portion of your Minijob income stays with you, and it helps maintain an unbroken employment record.
