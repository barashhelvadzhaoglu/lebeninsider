Renting an apartment in Germany is genuinely a stressful process. Especially in Munich and the surrounding area, prices are very high and the number of applicants is huge. In this post, as someone who recently moved here, I share what I experienced and learned step by step.

## Everything starts with an address: Anmeldung

For someone newly moving to Germany, the first task is finding a place to live. Because once you find an apartment, you go to the local registration office (*Bürgeramt*) and register your address with an **Anmeldung** (address registration), declaring "I live at this address." For this, you need a document from the landlord (*Wohnungsgeberbestätigung*); you are expected to register within 2 weeks of moving in.

Without this registration, you can do almost nothing:

- When you want to open a bank account, they ask for your address registration.
- Social benefits, procedures at official government offices—they all ask for your address first.

The reason is simple: everything here is sent by postal mail. Your bank card, the card's PIN code, letters from official authorities—they all arrive in your mailbox. That's why where you will live is critically important from day one.

### The catch-22

There is a vicious cycle here. You have just arrived from abroad and are about to sign an employment contract; the employer asks, "Where will you live?" You answer, "I'll temporarily stay in a hotel or an Airbnb." Maybe they are convinced. But if the hotel or Airbnb you stay at doesn't provide the document required for address registration, staying there serves no purpose in terms of registration. Therefore, even when looking for a temporary place, try to find somewhere where you can do an **Anmeldung**.

## My first apartment: a 4-month temporary rental

When I first arrived, I rented a temporary apartment for 4 months. I applied to tons of listings. Once I found this one, I wrote to the landlord; he initially asked for a deposit. I said, "I won't pay a deposit before signing the contract." We signed the contract, and then I paid the deposit. I came to Munich for a day or two, picked up the keys, signed the lease, and went back; then I returned a few days before starting my job.

The landlord was an Indian fellow. He had just had a baby and gone on parental leave (parental leave in Germany can last for months) back to India for a while; during that time, he rented out his apartment. The apartment had three rooms; he completely locked one room and stored his own belongings there. What remained was basically just a bed and a couch. There was no dishwasher, the fridge was very old, and the closets and beds were battered. We stayed there for 4 months, and during that entire time, I constantly searched listings.

Starting with a temporary flat makes a lot of sense in my opinion: you register your address, open your bank account, start work, and have a base while hunting for your real home.

## Listings and applications

From what I've seen, rentals generally go through **real estate agents** (*Makler*). There are a few major listing portals, and everyone applies to the exact same ads.

- **Timing:** Listings usually appear 2–4 months in advance. Meaning, the earliest move-in date for an apartment you see today might be 3–4 months out. Start searching early.
- **Application template:** I drafted an application template with the help of AI and immediately applied to listings using it. In the beginning, **I received zero replies**.
- **Salary information makes a huge difference:** The moment I included my salary details in the message, replies started coming in. I explained things like, "My gross salary is X; since I recently moved to Germany, I don't have a credit report yet or my score is low." Since the landlord's biggest question is "Can this person afford the rent?", providing this upfront helps tremendously.

As far as I understand, the process works like this: At least 100 people apply to an ad. The agent or landlord picks 8–10 people from among them and invites them to view the flat.

## Apartment viewing (Besichtigung)

Viewings can happen in different ways:

- Some invite everyone one by one.
- Some give a time slot: "You can come and view it on Saturday between 12:00 and 14:00."
- Some divide those two hours into 10-minute slots and take one person per slot.

You go inside, look around, check the layout and rooms. Then they ask, "Are you interested?" If you say yes, they pass it along to the landlord. Even if ten people are invited, maybe only three actually want to rent the place.

At the viewing, they usually have you fill out a form and sign it. Some hand you a printout with photos and details of the apartment. Those who want to rent usually present their **credit report (SCHUFA)** right there. Some prepare a CV-like one-pager with photos introducing themselves and their family. Since everything here runs on paper, you can hand this in printed form.

I went to see 8–10 apartments in Munich and neighboring towns. You have to convince the agent or landlord: "I really want this place." I don't know how effective that is, but it's worth trying.

### Speaking German is a factor

Speaking German can be one of the criteria. If the landlord doesn't speak English, communicating with the tenant will be difficult, so they prefer a German-speaking tenant. I don't view this as discrimination. Maybe that happens sometimes too, but it's an understandable request: when an issue arises, you need to be able to talk to the landlord.

## Apartments in Germany: things to know

- **Apartments are generally old:** Many were built after the two world wars; there are buildings from the 1930s, 60s, and 70s. Some landlords keep them well-maintained by renovating the interior, plumbing, and electrical systems every few years.
- **Room counts are calculated differently:** It's not like the "2+1" system in Turkey; the living room counts as a room. A "3-room apartment" (*3-Zimmer-Wohnung*) means what we know as 2 bedrooms + 1 living room.
- **Some apartments come without a kitchen:** Here, people don't fuss over marble countertops or elaborate cabinetry the way they do in Turkey, and some apartments are rented completely without a fitted kitchen. I was looking for a place with a kitchen, but I also viewed an apartment without one near my workplace. When I researched, I found ready-made kitchens including a fridge, stove, and dishwasher for 1,000–1,500 euros; if I recall correctly, they were sold with installments up to 24 months (they usually require a permanent employment contract and payslips). It's also possible to find second-hand kitchens on Kleinanzeigen; hardware stores also sell kitchens. Instead of marble, 4–5 cm thick waterproof coated particle board is used for countertops. I find it very sensible and practical; people spend serious money on this in Turkey, which I don't think is necessary.
- **Large complexes can be cheaper:** There are complexes with a few buildings of 6–8 units around a courtyard, as well as 40–50 unit apartment blocks. Because communal costs (heating, etc.) are divided among more units, incidental expenses can be lower in large complexes.

## Cold rent, warm rent, and utilities

In listings, you will see two rental figures:

- **Cold rent (Kaltmiete):** The base rent for the apartment alone.
- **Ancillary costs / utilities (Nebenkosten):** Expenses such as heating, hot water, building maintenance fees; paid separately.
- **Warm rent (Warmmiete):** Cold rent + Nebenkosten; the total amount you pay every month.

Nebenkosten are collected as a monthly advance payment and calculated once a year based on actual consumption. I explained this in my [post on daily life](/tr/almanya/yasam/almanyada-hayat-erken-baslar-erken-biter/).

## How much cash should you have ready?

This is a very crucial topic. Landlords typically ask for a security deposit (**Kaution**) equal to **3 months' cold rent**. On top of that, you pay the first month's rent. In other words, you need to have at least 4 months' worth of rent ready.

Here is my calculation for an apartment with a cold rent of 1,500 euros:

| Item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Deposit (3 × €1,500) | €4,500 |
| First month's cold rent | €1,500 |
| Nebenkosten (monthly) | €250–500 |
| **Total** | **€6,000–7,000** |

Kitchen, furniture, and moving costs can be added on top of this. Legally, the deposit cannot exceed 3 months of cold rent, and the tenant has the right to pay it in 3 installments; however, in practice, many landlords demand the full amount upfront.

## How did we furnish the apartment?

If you came from Turkey or another country, you can get some basic necessities from large stores like IKEA; if you don't have a car, factor in the delivery costs. We furnished part of our apartment through free or very cheap listings on **Kleinanzeigen**: appliances, furniture, children's items. I covered this in my [post on flea markets and second-hand in Germany](/en/germany/living/second-hand-shopping-flohmarkt-germany/).

## Circumstances that make finding an apartment easier

- **Corporate lease:** I saw this from a few people on YouTube: some companies rent the apartment directly and deduct the rent from your salary. Finding an apartment this way is much easier.
- **Bank guarantee:** Some companies have agreements with banks; if you work for that company, the bank provides a guarantee on your behalf. This also makes finding an apartment easier.

Apart from these, finding a home is very difficult for a newcomer to Germany. In my observation, demand in all cities has surged in recent years, partly due to the war in Ukraine.

## In a nutshell

1. First, find a place where address registration (Anmeldung) is possible; starting with a temporary home makes sense.
2. Start looking at listings 2–4 months in advance.
3. Clearly state your salary and situation in your application message.
4. Go to the viewing equipped with a credit report (SCHUFA) and an introductory profile sheet.
5. Have at least 4 months' worth of rent (€6,000–7,000 in Munich) ready.
6. Don't immediately dismiss apartments without a kitchen; a ready-made or second-hand kitchen is a viable solution.
