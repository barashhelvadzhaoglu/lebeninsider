*This article is part of my "Before Choosing a Career" series on career planning and choosing a profession. All parts of the series: [Career](/tr/kariyer/)*

In the previous part, I mentioned that when evaluating a profession, you need to look at how a day, a week, or a month actually unfolds in that role. Here, I want to give an example from my own field: IT.

My goal is to help those who are just starting out or considering stepping into this field visualize what things actually look like. Putting myself in their shoes, I remember there were many topics I couldn't grasp simply because I didn't know what some of the terms meant. That's why I'll also try to briefly explain the terminology.

Of course, I'm describing the work in very broad strokes here. I'm talking about a company's **IT department** (information technology department). In larger companies, there can be vastly different teams: a team that only develops mobile apps, a separate team building the website, and so on. The teams vary depending on the size of the company and its structure.

## How do requests come in?

Imagine you work at a company. Users from other departments usually submit their requests through specific software. These are known as a **help desk** or a **ticketing system**: every request is logged as a "ticket" and tracked. If no such software exists, employees reach out to IT staff via email or other channels with their requests. IT staff then fulfill those requests.

## The real job: keeping systems up and running

Assume there is an already operating system. When I say system, I mean everything that keeps a company running: camera infrastructure, network infrastructure, servers, software, hardware. IT staff carry out the work necessary to keep all of these running smoothly:

- When a new employee joins, the necessary setup is made, and a new email account is created.
- When an employee leaves, their email account, password, and user profile are deactivated.
- When a new department is established in the company, the required configurations are set up for that department.
- The systems used by the company get outdated and need renewal. You can think of this like upgrading from an older iPhone 15 to an iPhone 16: you back up the system and restore it on the new setup. Naturally, there might be slight differences and occasional issues along the way.
- Sometimes there are hardware problems, and a device simply stops working.

## The difference in scale

Let me put the scale into perspective for newcomers. At home, you have a laptop, and maybe 3–5 times a year you run into an issue with it. But imagine a company with 500 employees and 500 laptops, and each laptop malfunctions three times a year: that's **1,500 incidents** a year. Every single day, there is inevitably work waiting for you.

On top of that, technology is constantly evolving, and software and operating systems are regularly updated. You also need to keep up with them.

## Daily issues you encounter

In daily life, it's mostly routine issues:

- A user who cannot print from a printer
- A user who cannot send an email to a specific address
- A user who cannot access a website
- A user who cannot call a specific number through the PBX/phone system
- Users whose laptops break down, whose keyboards or mice don't work, or whose headsets and audio settings cause issues

In other words, there can be dozens or hundreds of different software and hardware issues, and all of them have to be resolved somehow.

## Levels: 1, 2, and 3

Companies usually structure this into tiers, such as Level 1, 2, and 3:

- **Level 1:** Employees at the beginning of their careers are expected to handle more basic, straightforward tasks.
- **Level 2:** Those who gain experience and develop their skills here move on to tasks requiring deeper technical know-how.
- **Level 3:** Highly experienced personnel. When a specific system needs to be replaced or overhauled, architectural approaches, designs, and planning—such as determining system requirements, which vendors can meet those needs, and what features should or shouldn't be included—are mostly handled by these teams.

## Working after hours: the reality of the industry

As IT professionals, we usually don't make major changes during business hours. While working from 8 to 5, we mainly handle routine, simple tasks. Changes that affect all users or impact the entire system are typically done **after hours**.

That's why there is a lot of after-hours work in this field, and anyone wanting to work in IT definitely needs to be able to adapt to this. Many companies don't pay extra for these overtime hours. Some offer compensatory time off that you can take on other days, while others offer nothing at all. Very rarely, there are companies that pay overtime wages. If you work for a company like that, you are truly lucky. That's just the reality of the industry.

Since many companies now provide remote access to their systems, you can do most after-hours maintenance remotely. However, for critical work that might disrupt your remote connection, you are expected to stay at the office and do the work on-site.

## Everything is "urgent"

Requests sent to you are almost always submitted as urgent, and you have to accommodate that urgency. Unfortunately, because many companies lack well-defined IT processes or approval workflows, you are expected to get to work immediately and quickly. You knock out the simple ones during the day, and for urgent tasks that impact the system, you work after hours.

## Meetings and teamwork

You have meetings throughout the day. Because you work as a team, teammates need to inform each other about what they're working on, what changes they've made, and what tasks they're dealing with. For this, there are daily and weekly meetings. Although it varies depending on the company and department, in a brief 15–20 minute daily meeting, for example, everyone spends about 2 minutes sharing what they've done, and then you get back to work. Sometimes these meetings drag on and delve into technical details.

Here's an example: Your manager gives you an assignment, and to complete it, you need to collaborate with someone from another team. A time slot where both of you are available is scheduled, and you work together during that window to complete the task.

Because you work in the IT department, you generally have to collaborate with other departments as well. So don't think, "I work in IT, my job is only with systems." You need to interact with many people, hold meetings together, and coordinate collaborative work. And companies prefer people who work well with a team, get along with others, and collaborate effectively across different departments.
