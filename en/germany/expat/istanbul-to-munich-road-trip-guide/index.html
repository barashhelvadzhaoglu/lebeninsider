Overview
#

Hello fellow expats and friends preparing to take their first steps into Germany! During our adventure of building a new life in Germany, we set out on a long family journey packed with our belongings: driving from Istanbul to Munich. This post serves as a guide for anyone considering this exact experience—hitting the road with two small kids, an LPG-powered car, and countless bags. If you are torn between flying and driving, or looking for practical solutions while moving to Germany, this detailed story of our experience is just for you.

Moving to Germany, especially coming from Turkey, brings along many logistical challenges. Transporting belongings, maintaining family routines, and travel comfort—especially for families with children—are some of the most critical aspects of this process. Keeping these factors in mind, we decided to drive our own car instead of dealing with airline baggage limits, layovers, and airport stress with young children. This decision offered a much more enjoyable and controllable travel experience than we ever expected.

So, when does this route make sense for you? If you, like us, are traveling with two young children, plan to carry lots of belongings, perhaps even furniture, bicycles, or pets, want a flexible travel schedule, and would love to see different sides of Europe, this guide will inspire you. Setting out in your own car can be budget-friendly and gives you the freedom to move at your own pace, stopping whenever and wherever you want.

What should motivate you to read this post? You will find everything here, from the border crossing procedures you might encounter while relocating to Germany, to the vignette (toll sticker) systems on European motorways, smart tactics for long-distance driving with kids, and even the camaraderie of Turkish truck drivers you meet along the way. I’ll share all the practical insights, experiences, and tips we gathered to make your journey less stressful, more affordable, and truly memorable. Fasten your seatbelts, let’s hit the road!

Step-by-Step Process
#

This roughly 1,500-kilometer journey from Istanbul to Munich requires navigating different countries, diverse road conditions, and varied experiences. Here is our step-by-step route and what you need to know:

Step 1: Istanbul – Bulgarian Border (Kapıkule/Hamzabeyli)
#

  • Where: Leaving Istanbul via the E80 motorway towards Edirne.
  • How: Exit through Kapıkule or Hamzabeyli border crossings on the Turkish side, and enter through Kapitan Andreevo or Lesovo on the Bulgarian side. Kapıkule is busier; Hamzabeyli can be much calmer.
  • How Long It Takes: About 2–3 hours from Istanbul to Kapıkule. Border wait times can range from 1 hour to 4–5 hours depending on congestion. It is generally calmer late at night or on weekdays.
  • Things to Watch Out For: Make sure your passports are valid and your Schengen visa is multiple-entry and currently valid. Keep your vehicle registration document and Green Card (international motor insurance certificate) handy. For detailed info on the Green Card: Green Card Insurance Guide.
  • Online / In-Person: Border crossings involve purely in-person passport and customs checks. No prior appointment is needed.

Step 2: Across Bulgaria (Kapitan Andreevo – Sofia – Calafat Border)
#

  • Where: After Kapitan Andreevo, take the A4 motorway to Sofia, then head towards the Vidin/Calafat border via the A2. However, our route went west from Sofia through a mountainous area directly towards the Calafat border crossing.
  • How: Once you enter Bulgaria, purchasing a vignette (toll sticker) is mandatory. You can buy one at kiosks right after the border or at the first gas stations. Options typically include 1 week, 1 month, or 1 year. Bulgarian motorways are generally in decent shape. However, part of the road from Sofia towards Romania’s Calafat border (heading to Vidin) is mountainous, winding, and not a divided highway.
  • How Long It Takes: 3–4 hours from Kapitan Andreevo to Sofia, and 3–4 hours from Sofia to Calafat (including the mountain section). Crossing Bulgaria altogether takes roughly 6–8 hours.
  • Things to Watch Out For: The mountain pass between Sofia and Calafat can be exhausting due to narrow, winding roads. We drove it at night with low visibility, which made it even more tiring. If possible, try driving this part in daylight—both for safety and the scenic views. Watch speed limits strictly in Bulgaria; speed traps are common.
  • Online / In-Person: The vignette can be purchased physically or online (bgtoll.bg). The border crossing is handled in-person over the New Europe Bridge (Calafat–Vidin Bridge), which requires a toll.

Step 3: Across Romania (Calafat – A1 Motorway – Nădlac/Arad Border)
#

  • Where: After entering Romania at Calafat, head north along the E79 route towards Pitești, then join the A1 motorway heading towards Budapest.
  • How: You must buy a vignette (rovinieta) upon entering Romania. They are sold at border stations or petrol pumps. In our experience, the quality of Romania’s interior roads pleasantly surprised us. In particular, the A1 motorway was a modern, broad, well-maintained European highway with several lanes.
  • How Long It Takes: 3–4 hours from Calafat until you connect to the A1, then 5–6 hours along the A1 to the Nădlac border. Crossing Romania takes roughly 8–10 hours in total.
  • Things to Watch Out For: You will find large supermarkets like Lidl and Kaufland along the route. These are great spots to take breaks and stock up on essentials. While LPG stations are common in Romania, some might be cash-only or require specific nozzle adapters. For details, check out: LPG in Europe Guide. Following Turkish semi-trucks can be a lifesaver here; truckers know the best, fastest, and smoothest routes.
  • Online / In-Person: Rovinieta can be purchased online (roviniete.ro) or physically. Border procedures are in-person.

Step 4: Hungary (Nădlac/Arad – Budapest – Hegyeshalom Border)
#

  • Where: Enter Hungary from Romania via the Nădlac border crossing and take the M43/M5 motorways toward Budapest. Then continue along the M1 motorway towards the Austrian border at Hegyeshalom.
  • How: Hungary also operates an e-vignette (e-matrica) system. You can buy it at border offices, petrol stations, or online (nemzetiutdij.hu). Hungarian motorways are very smooth and fast. We stayed overnight in Budapest, which was a fantastic rest stop on this long journey.
  • How Long It Takes: 2.5–3 hours from Nădlac to Budapest, and 1.5–2 hours from Budapest to Hegyeshalom. Total crossing through Hungary takes about 4–5 hours.
  • Things to Watch Out For: Petrol stations and service rest areas are frequent across Hungary. Budapest is ideal for an overnight stop thanks to its historic atmosphere and affordable lodging options.
  • Online / In-Person: The e-vignette is usually purchased online or registered digitally at gas stations. Border controls are done in-person.

Step 5: Austria (Hegyeshalom – Vienna – Passau Border)
#

  • Where: Enter Austria via the Hegyeshalom border, take the A4 motorway to Vienna, and then continue along the A1 motorway toward the German border (Passau direction).
  • How: A vignette (toll sticker) is mandatory in Austria as well. You can purchase it at the border, at the first fuel stops inside Austria, online (asfinag.at), or at automated kiosks. Short-term options (1-day, 10-day, 2-month) are available. Austrian motorways are top quality and very scenic.
  • How Long It Takes: About 1 hour from Hegyeshalom to Vienna, and 2–3 hours from Vienna to the German border near Passau. Crossing Austria takes roughly 3–4 hours in total.
  • Things to Watch Out For: Vignette enforcement cameras are everywhere and fines are hefty. Make sure to buy one and display it properly on the windshield if it’s a physical sticker. Comply strictly with speed limits and special tunnel regulations.
  • Online / In-Person: Vignettes are purchased online (asfinag.at) or in physical form.

Step 6: Germany (Passau – Munich)
#

  • Where: Enter Germany near Passau from Austria and follow the A3 and A92/A9 motorways to Munich.
  • How: Motorways (Autobahn) in Germany are free of charge for passenger cars, so no vignette or road toll is required. Road quality is top-notch, and many stretches have no blanket speed limit (though the recommended speed, Richtgeschwindigkeit, is 130 km/h).
  • How Long It Takes: 1.5–2 hours from Passau to Munich.
  • Things to Watch Out For: Even though parts of the Autobahn lack a mandatory maximum speed, limits are strictly enforced during poor weather or in construction zones. Pay attention to dynamic overhead signs. Always use the left lane strictly for overtaking and yield promptly to faster traffic approaching from behind.
  • Online / In-Person: The border crossing is generally seamless without systematic checks, though random border police spot checks can occur.

Other Expenses and Durations
#

  • Fuel (LPG / Petrol):

    • Distance: Approx. 1,500 km.
    • Fuel Consumption: Average of 8 l/100 km (mixed LPG/petrol). Roughly 120 liters of total fuel.
    • Fuel Prices: Across Europe, LPG averages around 0.70–1.00 EUR per liter, whereas petrol is roughly 1.50–1.90 EUR.
    • Estimated Fuel Cost:
      • For LPG: 120 l * 0.90 EUR = ~108 EUR
      • For Petrol: 120 l * 1.70 EUR = ~204 EUR
    • Note: LPG prices fluctuate significantly by country and even between individual stations. Romania and Bulgaria are comparatively cheaper, while Hungary and Austria lean pricier. You might also have to run on petrol briefly if an LPG adapter is missing.

  • Accommodation:

    • We stayed one night in Budapest. Airbnb or hotel prices vary depending on your style.
    • Estimated Cost: 60–120 EUR for one night. (We paid around 80 EUR.)

  • Food and Drinks:

    • Snacks from supermarkets, petrol station coffees, and a few proper meals along the way.
    • Estimated Cost: 50–100 EUR (depending on family size and preferences).

  • Hidden and Unexpected Expenses:

    • Translations / Apostilles: If you need extra documents along the route (like our citizenship appointment in Bulgaria), sworn translation fees can run 20–50 EUR per document.
    • Post / Shipping: In case you need to mail paperwork while traveling.
    • Photos: Biometric passport photos if needed.
    • Car Maintenance: Pre-trip servicing (oil, fluids, brake pads, tire check). While strictly speaking not a “travel cost,” it is essential for safety.
    • Roadside Assistance: Annual membership for ADAC or a similar auto club (around 50–100 EUR).
    • Kids’ Entertainment: Small toys, travel books, offline tablet downloads.

Total Estimated Cost and Time:

  • Total Estimated Cost (Minimum): Around 350 – 550 EUR (depending on fuel type, accommodation, and food choices).
  • Total Estimated Travel Time: Around 22–23 hours of non-stop driving. We completed this journey across 2 full days.
    • Day 1: Istanbul into central Romania (around 12–14 hours on the road).
    • Day 2: Romania to Munich (including the Budapest overnight, roughly 10–12 hours of driving).
    • When traveling with small kids, stretching the itinerary over 3 days can offer a much calmer rhythm.

Practical Tips
#

To help make this long drive from Istanbul to Munich as smooth and enjoyable as possible, here are the hands-on tips we picked up:

  1. Check Green Card Scope and Plan Alternate Routes: Examine your Green Card policy thoroughly before leaving. Check which countries are included in coverage. In our case, our budget-friendly policy only covered EU member states, which forced us to take the Romania route rather than driving through Serbia. Always verify your territorial coverage well before departure. Learn more in our Green Card Insurance Guide.
  2. LPG Adapters and Refueling Strategy: If your car runs on LPG/autogas, prepare for varying filler nozzle adapters across Europe (Dish, ACME, Bajonet, Euronozzle). Our Honda Civic needed different connectors, and some rural stations lacked adapters to lend. Keep a personal set of universal brass adapters in your glove box and verify what standard each country uses ahead of time. Note that in some European countries, you have to pump LPG yourself. Our LPG in Europe Guide covers all of this in detail.
  3. The “Follow the Turkish Trucks” Tactic: Especially in unfamiliar territory like Romania, tailing Turkish-plated semi-trucks is a surprisingly reliable strategy. Long-haul truckers drive these corridors countless times a year and know the smoothest roads, speed trap locations, and safe rest stops. They often provide better real-time navigation than any GPS app.
  4. Night Driving with Small Kids: When traveling with little ones, night driving can be an absolute lifesaver. We set off right around our kids’ bedtime (20:00–21:00). While they slept soundly in the back, we covered the most demanding stretches quietly. When fatigue hits around 2:00 or 3:00 AM, pull into a large, well-lit service station, lock the doors, and nap in the car for 3–4 hours. Waking up refreshed lets you continue without ever disrupting the children’s sleep rhythms.
  5. Don’t Forget Vignettes and Verify Plate Numbers: Vignettes are mandatory on motorways across Bulgaria, Romania, Hungary, and Austria. Buy them immediately after border posts or at the very first service area. If purchasing digital vignettes, double-check that your license plate number and country code are entered correctly. Driving without a vignette triggers steep automated fines. In Austria, ensure any physical toll sticker is placed correctly on the windshield.
  6. Smooth Border Crossings: Keep all passports, visas, vehicle registration papers, insurance cards, and driving licenses grouped in an easily accessible folder. Crossings between EU/Schengen borders (like Hungary to Austria) rarely involve full checks, but random spot checks are always possible.
  7. Tips for Settling in Germany: A few reminders for when you finally arrive: you can use your Turkish driving license for up to six months after registering your residence (Anmeldung), after which you must convert it to a German license. Check our guide on the Driver’s License Conversion Process in Germany. If you hold a Bulgarian license or are looking into EU driving privileges, read our post Can You Drive in Germany with a Bulgarian License?. Finally, securing long-term housing is another huge milestone—our Renting an Apartment in Germany Guide will walk you through that step.

Our Personal Experience
#

Driving from Istanbul to Munich was an unforgettable bundle of memories from start to finish. To be completely honest, the prospect of tackling 1,500 km in our own car with two small kids (aged 1 and 5) felt quite intimidating at first. We repeatedly asked ourselves, “Wouldn’t flying just be easier?” But the mountain of luggage we needed to transport, paired with the sheer stress of managing toddlers through airports and luggage claims, pushed us toward this adventurous choice. Looking back, we are so glad we did it!

One of the most pleasant surprises was the quality of Romanian motorways. Setting off with typical Western European expectations, we worried Romania might consist entirely of bumpy village roads. Instead, the A1 motorway proved wide, impeccably maintained, fast, and on par with the German Autobahn. That stretch turned out to be one of the smoothest, most enjoyable parts of the entire trip.

Our night driving tactic worked wonders. Eating up miles while the kids slept meant their biological clocks stayed undisturbed, and we avoided hours of “Are we there yet, Daddy?” Pulling into a service station around 02:00 to sleep in the car might sound uncomfortable at first, but it was surprisingly restful. Because the kids were in deep sleep, these brief stops didn’t disrupt them in the slightest.

The camaraderie of Turkish truck drivers on the road gave us memories we still cherish. We saw so many Turkish license plates through Romania and Hungary that it felt like driving in a moving community. During a fuel stop, a brief chat over tea with a Turkish truck driver parked next to us boosted our spirits and provided great tips about road conditions ahead. That warm sense of solidarity on the long road to a new life really touched our hearts.

The hardest part was undoubtedly the mountainous, winding stretch between Sofia and Calafat in Bulgaria. Navigating narrow curves in the dead of night through patchy fog was truly draining. On our return trip, however, seeing active highway and tunnel construction gave us hope that this section will soon be far easier to navigate.

Our one-night stop in Budapest gave us the exact breathing room we needed halfway through. A quick glimpse of the city’s historic charm, sleeping in a real bed, and waking up refreshed confirmed that breaking up the trip was the right move. If your schedule allows, I wholeheartedly recommend an overnight stay in Budapest.

All in all, this road trip did more than show us the changing faces of Europe. It brought our family closer together, proved we could overcome unexpected hurdles, and made stepping into a brand-new life feel like a shared triumph. Far better than we could have imagined, that journey was the perfect prologue to our new beginning in Germany.

Frequently Asked Questions
#

Q: Why not take the route through Serbia? A: While Serbia looks shorter on paper, budget Green Card vehicle insurance purchased on the Bulgarian border often only covers EU member states. Since Serbia is outside the EU, driving through would require purchasing expensive supplemental border insurance or risking refusal at the checkpoint.

Q: Are roads in Romania actually good? A: Yes, particularly Romania’s modern A1 motorway, which is wide, fast, and very well maintained. While the mountainous Bulgarian segment between Sofia and Calafat can be demanding, Romanian motorways meet high European standards and offer a very smooth drive.

Q: Is sleeping at a petrol station with kids safe? A: Large, well-lit, 24-hour motorway service areas with regular truck parking are generally safe. Always lock your car doors and crack windows just slightly for ventilation without letting outside noise overwhelm you. Well-known chains like OMV, MOL, and Lukoil across Romania, Hungary, and Austria are reliable choices.

Q: Is an International Driving Permit mandatory? A: In Germany and across the EU, your standard Turkish driver’s license is recognized for short-term visits and during your first six months of official residency. An International Driving Permit is not legally mandatory, though some drivers carry one to simplify communication during routine roadside checks.

Q: Where do I buy the Austrian vignette, and what happens if I skip it? A: You can purchase the Austrian vignette at fuel stations near the Hungarian-Austrian border, at the first service plazas inside Austria, or online via asfinag.at. Motorway cameras strictly enforce tolls, and fines for driving without a vignette are steep (typically 120–300 EUR). Never risk driving without one.

Q: Are credit cards accepted everywhere along the route? A: Major fuel chains, supermarkets, and motorway service plazas accept international credit cards without issue. However, smaller rural shops, independent stations, or certain LPG refill points may only take local cash. Carrying a moderate amount of Euros in cash is strongly recommended.

Q: What safety precautions should I take before such a long road trip? A: Give your vehicle a thorough mechanical checkup before leaving (engine oil, coolant, brake pads, tire pressure, and tire tread depth). Keep an EU-compliant first aid kit, warning triangle, reflective safety vests for all passengers inside the cabin, and a spare tire. Signing up for international roadside assistance (like ADAC) provides invaluable peace of mind in the event of an unexpected breakdown.
