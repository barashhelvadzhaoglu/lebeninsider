Sharing Culture in Germany: Doorstep Gifts and Silent Social Consciousness
#

Once, it was a meaningless sight to me: a child’s stroller in good condition, left in front of an apartment door. On it, a handwritten note: “Zu verschenken” (To give away as a gift). No one waits, no one sees the person who takes it. But the next day, the stroller is gone. As if an invisible hand delivered it to someone in need. This is precisely the simplest and most striking form of sharing culture in Germany. An unpretentious, silent, yet deeply ingrained social consciousness.

About Germany: The Society that Forms the Foundation of Sharing Culture
#

Germany, with a population of approximately 84 million in Central Europe, is one of the continent’s most populous countries. Bordering nine different countries, such as France to the west and Poland to the east, it is known for its industrial power, cultural richness, and disciplined way of life. Its capital is Berlin, but major cities like Munich, Hamburg, and Cologne are also important economic and cultural centers.

Germany’s history has a complex past, stretching from the Roman Empire to the Holy Roman Empire, from reform movements to the two world wars of the modern era. This deep history has laid the groundwork for certain values to take root in German society. Especially the post-war reconstruction process ensured that great importance was given to the efficient use of resources, social order, and prosperity. Today, Germany is not only one of the world’s leading economies but also a pioneer in environmental awareness and sustainability. This awareness has played a vital role in the flourishing and rooting of the sharing culture.

German cities blend modern architecture with historical buildings. Its people are generally rule-abiding, punctual, and organized. Although they may seem distant in initial encounters with foreigners, you discover over time that they can be warm and helpful. A high level of awareness prevails, especially in matters that provide social benefit. This fosters a sharing culture that takes a stand against consumerism and is reluctant to throw away usable items.

Why is this sharing culture so widespread in Germany? Because here, instead of the “throw away the old, buy the new” mentality, the philosophy of “make use of what’s still functional” is paramount. This is not just a matter of financial savings but also a silent expression of environmental responsibility and social solidarity. When you step into Germany, you will feel this hidden economy and silent subconscious agreement, and perhaps realize how humane and practical this system, which might initially seem cold, actually is.

Observation Points of Sharing Culture: Where to Meet, Where to Share?
#

In Germany, instead of traditional “places to visit,” there are many spots where you can observe and even experience this sharing culture. These places most concretely demonstrate society’s understanding of sustainability and solidarity.

Doorstep Sharing (Zu verschenken)
#

In almost every city in Germany, you can encounter items left with a “Zu verschenken” note at apartment entrances, next to trash bins, or on sidewalks. This is perhaps the most common and accessible form of sharing culture. An old bicycle, unused kitchenware, children’s toys, sometimes even a sofa or a desk… No price, no questions asked. Those in need take it, those who don’t, pass by. When we first moved to Munich, we acquired many of our kitchen items and even our first iron from this doorstep economy. This is an unpretentious way to extend the life of items and reduce waste production. Although there isn’t a specific point called Munich Doorstep Sharing Points, you are likely to encounter such examples while walking anywhere in the city.

Giveboxes and Tauschregale (Exchange Shelves)
#

In many German cities, especially in neighborhoods with a high concentration of young people and environmentally conscious communities, you can find small public boxes or shelves called Givebox (Google Maps) or Tauschregale (exchange shelves) (Google Maps). These are usually made of wood, in the form of a small hut or a shelf unit. Books, small household items, toys, and sometimes even clothes are left inside. The basic principle here is “take-give.” You can leave an item and take something you need. They are generally open 24 hours and are not under any supervision, relying entirely on social trust. We have often left toys our children outgrew or books we read, and taken other books.

Umsonstläden (Free Shops)
#

In some cities, there are places called Umsonstladen (free shops) (Google Maps), operated by volunteers. As the name suggests, all products here are free. People bring items they no longer need to these shops, and anyone in need can take whatever they wish. They offer a wide range of products, from clothes to kitchenware, books to toys. These shops usually have specific opening hours and, in addition to promoting a sustainable lifestyle, also provide significant support to low-income individuals.

Repair Cafes (Repair Cafés)
#

Although not direct item sharing, the Repair Cafe (Google Maps) culture, with its “repair instead of discard” philosophy, is an important part of the sharing culture. In these cafés, volunteer experts help you repair many things, from broken household appliances to your bicycle, from clothes to electronic devices. The aim is to prevent waste generation, educate people on repair skills, and increase social interaction. These events, regularly organized in many cities, strengthen the sense of community and encourage more efficient use of resources.

Kleinanzeigen (Formerly eBay Kleinanzeigen)
#

Kleinanzeigen is one of Germany’s largest second-hand classifieds platforms and is the digital face of this sharing culture. Among millions of listings, the “Zu verschenken” (to give away as a gift) category holds a special place. Here, even items too large or valuable for doorstep sharing can change hands for free. Furniture, electronics, white goods, sports equipment… A wide range of products is offered. When we moved to Munich, we also acquired many of our furniture items, especially my office chair and small storage units, for free via Kleinanzeigen. The platform is extremely simple to use: search, send a message, meet, and pick up. Mutual respect and understanding among users are quite high.

Accommodation: Sustainable Options and Neighborhood Recommendations
#

You can stay in any city in Germany to experience the sharing culture, but large cities offer the most vibrant examples of this culture. Choosing accommodation options that align with sustainability awareness will complete this experience.

  • Motel One München Sendlinger Tor (Google Maps | Booking.com): A modern and affordable hotel chain in the center of Munich. Its proximity to the city center makes it ideal for exploring sharing points. Rooms are generally compact and functional. Price range: 80-150 EUR/night.
  • Scandic Berlin Potsdamer Platz (Google Maps | Booking.com): An eco-friendly hotel in Berlin with sustainability certifications. It offers easy access to the city’s main attractions and has modern amenities. Price range: 100-200 EUR/night.
  • Superbude Hotel & Hostel St. Pauli (Google Maps | Booking.com): A young and dynamic place in Hamburg combining both hotel and hostel concepts. Its common areas are decorated with second-hand items and offer a creative atmosphere. A community-focused accommodation experience reflecting the sharing culture. Price range: 60-120 EUR/night.
  • Smart Stay Hotel Frankfurt (Google Maps | Booking.com): An affordable and centrally located hotel in Frankfurt. It provides easy access to sharing points and transportation networks. Price range: 70-130 EUR/night.

Which Neighborhood is Better to Stay In?

To experience the sharing culture, it is best to choose lively neighborhoods in large cities where local residents live densely. For example, areas like Schwabing or Haidhausen in Munich, Kreuzberg or Neukölln in Berlin, and St. Pauli or Altona in Hamburg are places where you are more likely to encounter doorstep sharing, Giveboxes, and Kleinanzeigen meetups. These areas also have a stronger sense of community, with small independent shops and local cafés. Since city centers are generally more touristy and corporate, it would be beneficial to head towards more local neighborhoods to observe more “organic” examples of sharing culture.

How to Get There? Transportation to Germany and City Travel
#

Getting to Germany is quite easy, and the country also has a developed transportation network within its borders.

Transportation from Turkey to Germany
#

The most common way to travel from Turkey to Germany is by plane. There are direct flights from major cities like Istanbul, Ankara, and Izmir to many large German cities such as Munich, Frankfurt, Berlin, Hamburg, and Cologne.

Domestic Travel in Germany
#

The most common and efficient method of transportation within Germany is by train (Deutsche Bahn - DB).

  • Train (Deutsche Bahn - DB): Germany has an extensive train network connecting almost every city. High-speed trains (ICE) provide quick travel between major cities, while regional trains (RE, RB) are slower but run frequently. Ticket prices can be more affordable when booked early or with regional tickets.
    • Deutschlandticket (Germany Ticket): Offering unlimited travel on regional trains, buses, trams, and subways throughout Germany for 49 EUR per month, this ticket is an excellent option for those who want to visit different cities and towns to observe the sharing culture. You can check out our article: Exploring Around Munich with the Deutschlandticket.
  • Bus (like FlixBus): Intercity buses are generally more affordable than trains, but travel times are longer.
  • Car Rental: While driving in Germany is comfortable, finding parking can be difficult and expensive, especially in large cities. Additionally, it may be necessary to pay attention to topics such as LPG Guide in Europe and Green Card Insurance Guide.

City Transportation
#

Public transport systems in German cities are highly developed.

  • Subway (U-Bahn), Tram (Tram), and Bus: Each city has its own integrated public transport system. You can use different modes of transport with a single ticket. Tickets can usually be purchased hourly, daily, or weekly.
  • Bicycle: Bicycle use is quite common, especially in cities with flat terrain (like Berlin, Hamburg), and the network of bicycle paths is well-developed. Bike-sharing systems (like Call a Bike) are also available.
  • Walking: City centers are generally suitable for exploring on foot. You can discover the best examples of sharing culture (doorstep sharing, Giveboxes) while walking.

Our Experience: The Simplicity and Expectations of Heartfelt Sharing
#

When we first arrived in Germany, we encountered a model very different from the understanding of “mutual aid” in Turkey. In Turkey, help is usually given directly, face-to-face, and often with an expectation of “thanks.” Here, however, it was the opposite: a silent, unpretentious, and anonymous sharing.

Initially, this situation seemed a bit cold and distant to me. A process of item exchange where there’s no need to thank for an item left at the doorstep, perhaps even unseen by anyone… This was quite different when I considered the importance we place on human relationships, our perception of hospitality and generosity. But over time, I understood that this was actually a much deeper form of respect. Giving something without making the other person feel indebted, without expecting gratitude, simply with the thought “may it be useful to someone in need.” This is an approach that doesn’t harm the recipient’s dignity, offering them space to meet their needs independently.

For us, this sharing culture became an inseparable part of our expat life in Munich. When we first settled into our home, we acquired many basic items via Kleinanzeigen, either for a symbolic fee or completely free. From baby room furniture to kitchen utensils, and even a few decorative objects… This not only provided financial savings but also helped us understand the social fabric in Germany.

As our children grew, we also started giving their toys and clothes to Giveboxes or as “Zu verschenken” via Kleinanzeigen. This wasn’t just about getting rid of excess items at home, but also knowing that those items might bring a smile to another child’s face. This cycle made us a part of this silent social contract. Especially against the increasing consumerism during New Year’s Celebrations in Germany, this sharing culture showed us that a more sustainable life is possible.

This experience is also closely related to Germany’s general culture of trust. Items being left at doorsteps without interference, Giveboxes operating unsupervised, are indicators of the fundamental trust this society has in one another. Even in challenging experiences like Being Fired in Germany, trust in the system’s operation creates a belief that the process will be fairer.

In conclusion, the sharing culture in Germany is not just an act of “giving away items,” but also a philosophy of life built upon deep environmental awareness, social respect, and mutual trust. This is perhaps one of the warmest and most humane aspects of this society, which might appear cold from the outside. Acquiring such cultural information, along with practical guides like Guide to Finding Rent in Germany, will make the adaptation process to life in Germany much more enjoyable and meaningful.

Frequently Asked Questions
#

How widespread is the sharing culture in Germany?
#

It is quite widespread. It has become an important part of daily life, especially through doorstep sharing, the Kleinanzeigen platform, and Giveboxes.

Is there a cost to taking free items?
#

No, there is no direct cost. However, as a social courtesy, smiling or politely thanking (if you encounter the person giving the item) is sufficient. It is generally an anonymous process.

What kind of items are shared for free in Germany?
#

Many different types of items can be shared, from furniture to electronics, books to children’s toys, kitchenware to clothes. The important thing is that the item is still in usable condition.

Can foreigners also benefit from this sharing culture?
#

Absolutely. The sharing culture makes no distinction based on ethnic origin or social status. Anyone in need can benefit equally from this system.

Is this sharing culture legal?
#

Yes, it is completely legal. Items left at doorsteps are usually placed before “sperrmüll” (bulky waste) collection days or simply left as “unclaimed.” Platforms like Kleinanzeigen enable item exchange through entirely legal means.

Is sharing culture only present in large cities?
#

No, examples of this culture can be found in both large cities and smaller towns across Germany. Especially in rural areas, more direct sharing can occur through neighborly relations.

How does this culture contribute to sustainability?
#

By preventing items from going to waste, it reduces the amount of waste, decreases consumption of new products, and ensures more efficient use of resources. This contributes positively to both the environment and the economy.

Are there similar cultures in other countries?
#

Yes, while similar “gift economy” or “second-hand” sharing practices exist in different cultures, Germany’s silent, anonymous, and unorganized (like doorstep) yet widespread system is unique.
